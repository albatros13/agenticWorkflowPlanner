Norms and rules for providing a residence permit for foreign nationals in the Netherlands
In this chapter, we present a case study for making norms and rules by interpreting legal sources concerning an example based on the Dutch General administrative law act (the procedural framework for Dutch administrative law).1

This research project began as a search for a method to make shared interpretation of legal sources related to administrative procedures in the Netherlands, particularly in the context of immigration. 
How to extract acts, claims and facts from normative text
We will be looking for sources that describe how people can lodge a request to the Dutch government, and how the government can decide on these requests. In section 8.1 we will study the procedural aspects of this matter, in section 8.2 we will substantiate the case with an example on making decisions concerning applications to provide a temporary regular residence permit according to the Dutch Aliens act.

In both sections we will use the five steps:
    1. setting the task (the case),
    2. collecting sources,
    3. interpreting sources,
    4. applying norms and rules to the case.
1 Lodging a request for making a decision by an administrative authority according to the General administrative law act
1.1 Setting the task
The task studied in this section is: ‘Make a legal framework for lodging a request to the Dutch government to make a decision, and for making the requested decision’.
1.2 Collecting sources
We will make an interpretation of that part of the Dutch General administrative law act2 that regulates the act of lodging a request to an administrative authority for making a ‘written decision’ (an ‘order’).

The collection of sources was an iterative process. We started by collecting Article 4:1 and 4:2 General administrative law act on lodging an application in writing and the things an application must contain. From there, sources were added based on interpretation (the adding of Article 1:3 (3) was the result of a search for information on the question what is an ‘application’) and comments from domain experts (addressing issues like the difference between a complete ‘application’ and an application that needs to be supplement by the applicant).

The set of sources presented here is the current set. Collecting additional sources can be necessary when existing sources are changed, new sources are added, or when the interpretation of existing sources changed.

The General administrative law act is a procedural law that governs the relationship between administrative authorities and the public. The law was enacted on 1 January 1994. All articles in this analysis were in that original version of the law, except for Article 3:46 which was added on 1 January 1998 and Article 4:5 that was changed on 1 July 2004.3 This shows that the general framework of the General administrative law act is rather stable.

1.3 Interpreting sources
In order to make a normative interpretation a regulatory source we qualify sentences in the source as an act or a claim. These sources are represented in the source slot of a frame, see e.g. figure 7.4, 7.5 and 8. 1 and 8.2. 

The next step is to qualify words or phrases as ‘facts’ that play a role in these ‘acts’. The core of an act is the combination of an ‘action’, the ‘actor’ that has the power to execute the ‘action’, the ‘object’ that the ’action’ is referring to (the thing that is acted upon), and the ‘recipient’ of the ’result’ of the action (the liable party).

The ‘act frame’ is completed by adding a ‘precondition’ (a Boolean expression of the ‘condition’ required for a valid ‘action’) and a ‘result’ or ‘postcondition’ (the ‘result’ of an ‘act’ is the creation or termination of one or more ‘objects’ or ‘duties’).

If a ‘duty’ is created as the ‘result’ of an ‘action’, it needs to be expanded to a ‘claim’ (a.k.a. a ‘claim-duty relation’). Meaning that it must have a ‘claimant’ and a ‘duty holder’. These can be the ‘actor’ or the ‘recipient’ of the act that creates the ‘duty’, but this is not necessarily the case. It can also be a third party.

Let’s make a legal framework for lodging a request for an order made by an administrative authority by interpretation of the source collection in section 8.1.2.
Lodging a request by for an order by an administrative authority
The act of ‘lodging a request for an order’ is not explicitly found the General administrative law act. Article 1:3(3) General administrative law act gives a definition of what is an ‘application’, but it does not explicitly contain the ‘action’ of lodging a request itself.
The action
Article 1:3 (3) General administrative law act contains the definition of an ‘application’, it lacks the mention of the ‘action’ needed to create an ‘application’.5 That ‘action’ (to ‘lodge’) can be found in Article 4:1 General administrative law act.
The actor
Who is the ‘actor’ that lodges the request? Article 1:3 (3) General administrative law act includes the reference to an ‘interested party’, Article 4:1 General administrative law act mentions an ‘applicant’.

The ‘applicant’ only becomes the applicant after lodging the request. Before that moment he is at best an ‘aspirant applicant’. However, the ‘interested party’ does not need to be the ‘applicant’, see Article 3:41(1) General administrative law act. It can be a third party, a party that is not the applicant but has an interest in the request, e.g., a neighbor of the applicant of a building permit.

To emphasize the person lodging the application, we settle for the ‘applicant’ as the ‘actor’ who ‘lodges’ the ‘request for an order’. An important aspect of our method for te interpretation of regulatory sources is that implicit knowledge is laid down explicitly in the interpretation in the interpretations see the annotation slot in table 8.1.
The object
What is lodged by the actor, is the ‘request for an order’.
The recipient
Article 1:3(1) General administrative law act states that the ‘order’ is “a written decision of an administrative authority”. Since this act is about the ‘request for an order’, the ‘recipient’ of that request must be the one that creates the order, i.e. the ‘administrative authority’.
The precondition
The possibility to lodge a ‘request for an order’ by an ‘administrative authority’ is supposed to be open for everyone. One needs not to be a citizen of the Netherlands, nor to be lawful resident. All that is required, according to Article 1:3(1) General administrative law act, is that the applicant is an interested party.

If there is no interest, there is no need to use resources for making a decision and laying the decision down in an order.
The result
If a ‘request for an order’ is lodged validly, the ‘result’ is the creation of an ‘application’. This needs not be a complete ‘application’. An incomplete ‘application’ can only be disregarded by an ‘administrative authority’ after the ‘applicant’ has been given the opportunity to send supplemental information (within a set time limit), see Article 4:5 General administrative law act. 6

The duties of the ‘administrative authority’. At the same time, a set of ‘duties’ are created for the ‘administrative authority’ involved as well, i.e.:

The core-act of the act to disregard an application
The source of the ‘action’ (‘to disregard’), the ‘actor’ (an ‘administrative authority’) and the ‘object’ (the ‘application’) can all be found in the introductory clause of Article 4:5(1) General administrative law act. The ‘recipient’ (the ‘applicant’) can be found in Article 4:5(1)(a) General administrative law act.
The precondition
The ‘precondition’ for ‘disregarding an application’ is given in Article 4:5(1)(a), (b) and (c) General administrative law act. Applications can be disregarded if:
    (a) they do not comply complied with any legal requirement for processing the application, 
    (b) they have been refused based on Article 2:15 General administrative law act, that is about digital applications, or
    (c) if the applicant has not provided the information and documents needed for the assessment of the application.

An application can only be disregarded after the applicant has had the opportunity to complete the application and the decision to disregard the application is announced to the applicant within four weeks after the application has been supplemented or after the period set for this has expired unused.

Article 4:5(2) and (3) General administrative law act contain additional specifications for the ‘conditions’ described in Article 4:5(1)(c). They concern:
    • information and documents written in a foreign language that are not translated, and 
    • information and documents of a substantial size or complexity that are not summarized.
The result
The ‘result’ of ‘disregarding an application’ is the ‘order to disregard the application’. But before the ‘order’ comes into effect, the ‘recipient’ must be notified. This is a separate act.
Notifying an applicant on an order made by an administrative authority
The core act of notifying an applicant on an order can be found in Articles 1:3(1), 3:40 and 3:41(1) General administrative law act., see table 8.4.
The action
The ‘action’ can be derived from the word ‘notified’ in Article 3:40 General administrative law act.
The actor
The ‘actor’ is the ‘administrative authority’ since he makes the order, and therefore can make a notification, see Article 1:3(1) General administrative law act.
The object
The ‘object’ of the notification is the ‘order’. The notification concerns the ‘order’ (Article 3:40 General administrative law act).
The recipient
The ‘recipient’ is the applicant, see Article 3:41(1) General administrative law act. The notification can, according to Article 3:41(1) General administrative law act, also be send to other interested parties. The context in which this happens is not laid down in the General administrative law act and is therefore not further analyzed here.
The precondition
The ‘precondition’ for the notification is that the ‘administrative authority’ is ‘competent to decide on the application’, as is stated in Article 4:1 General administrative law act.

The result
The ‘result’ of the notification is that a ‘notification of the order’ is created, and that the ‘order has taken effect’, based on Article 3:40 General administrative law act.
Since the requested order has now taken effect, the ‘duties’ related to the lodging of the application, are now no longer relevant. The application is taken care of, the ‘duties’ serve no purpose. The notification on the order therefore terminates al these duties.7

The application itself is also terminated. If not, it would be possible to create two (different) written decisions (‘orders’) on the same application. An application could both be ‘granted’ and ‘rejected’. To prevent this, the ‘application’ is terminated by the ‘notification’ of the ‘order’.

If one wants to change the ‘order’ after it has been notified, the ‘order’ should first be ‘revoked’. Then a new decision can be made.
1.4 Applying norms and rules to the case
In this section we have made a frame-based model of lodging a request for an order, made by an ‘administrative authority’ based on the General administrative law act.

The framework shows how:
    • the ‘lodging’ of a ‘request for an order’ becomes an ‘application’,
    • how the ‘lodging’ of a ‘request for an order’ ‘results’ in ‘duties’ for the ‘applicant’ and for the ‘administrative authority’ that receives the request, 
    • it shows the ‘condition’ in which such requests can be ‘disregarded’, and 
    • describes the act of ‘notifying’ the ‘applicant’ that an order has been made.

However, to decide in specific cases the procedural framework laid down in the General administrative law act is insufficient. We will need substantive law, since that contains the descriptions of administrative decision that can be applied for, and the ‘conditions’ for granting or rejecting the application. 

In the next section we present an example based on procedures described in the Dutch Aliens act.

From Article 14(1) Aliens act, introductory clause and under (a) can be ‘granted’, ‘rejected’ or ‘disregarded’. Here we will analyze the ‘granting’ (action) of such an ‘application’.
The actor
The actor, according to Article 14(1) Aliens act introductory clause is ‘Our Minister’. Article 1 Aliens act introductory clause and under Our Minister specifies that ‘Our Minister’ is referring to ‘Our Minister of Security and Justice’ in the context of the Aliens act.
The object
The ‘object’ of this act can be found in Article 14(1)(a) in combination with the heading of division 3.3 of the Aliens act (The regular residence permit). The ‘object’ that is granted is the ‘application to provide temporary regular residence permit’.
The recipient
The ‘recipient’ of the act cannot be found in Article 14(1) Aliens act. However, in Article 8 Aliens act, introductory clause and under (a) we find that “[t]he alien only has lawful residence in the Netherlands: (a) on the basis of a temporary residence permit as referred to in Article 14”.

We add Article 8 Aliens act, introductory clause and under (a) to the sources of the act to ‘grant a temporary regular residence permit’ and qualify the ‘alien’ as the ‘recipient’ of this act.

 Article 26(1) Aliens act states that a regular residence permit is granted from the day on which the alien has demonstrated that he meets all conditions, but not earlier than from the day on which the application was received.

Article 26(1) is the only ‘condition’ mentioned in the Aliens Act for granting applications for providing temporary regular residence permits. 

We will elaborate the ‘fact frame’ ‘regular residence permit is granted from the day on which the alien has demonstrated that he meets all conditions’ shortly, but first we will address the other elements in the ‘precondition’, and the ‘result’ of this ‘act frame’.

The ‘precondition’ of the act consists of two conditions:
    1. ‘regular residence permit is granted from the day on which the alien has demonstrated that he meets all conditions’,
    2. ’residence permit granted earlier than from the day on which the application was received’.

The second ‘condition’ is rather straight forward. This ‘condition’ is comprehensible for people and easily translated into machine readable constraints. 
 
The first ‘condition’ however, is more complicated because it is unclear what is ‘all conditions’. What conditions should the alien meet?

The Aliens act does not contain any explicit ‘conditions’ for ‘granting’ a residence permit, it does contain ‘conditions’ for ‘rejecting’ and ‘disregarding’ applications.

We will analyze these acts first, then we will elaborate the ‘condition’ ‘regular residence permit is granted from the day on which the alien has demonstrated that he meets all conditions’14 giving an overview of the ‘conditions’ for ‘granting an application for providing a temporary regular residence permit’.
The result
The ‘result’ of the ‘granting an application to provide a temporary regular residence permit’ consists of multiple components.

Based on Article 14(1) introductory clause and under (a) Aliens act in combination with Article 1:3(3) General administrative law act the ‘order to grant an application to provide a temporary regular residence permit’ is created.

The ‘application to provide a temporary regular residence permit’ is terminated when the order is made, preventing the creation of contradicting orders.

Furthermore, a set of ‘duties’ is created based on Article 9(1), 14(3) and (4) Aliens act:
    • Article 9(1) requires that a document proving lawful residence is provide to the alien that is granted a residence permit, i.e. to provide the residence permit, not only the order stating that it has been granted,
    • in Article 14(3) it is laid down that a temporary regular residence permit is granted under restrictions related to the purpose of residence.
    • Article 14 (4) requires the determination of the period of validity of the residence permit, that may not exceed a period of 5 years.

For terminating these ‘duties’ new ‘act-	frames’  must be made by interpreting additional sources.

The rejection of an ‘application to provide a temporary regular residence permit’ does not result in the creation of additional duties.

Only in exceptional cases the reasons for having an ‘entry ban’, ‘flagged for the purpose of refusing entry’ or being ‘declared undesirable’ do not qualify for rejection based on posing ‘a danger to public order or national security’. No domain expert we asked could come up with an example.

3 Conclusion
In this chapter we have presented examples for making legal frameworks for lodging requests to the Dutch governments, deciding on these requests in general, and deciding on applications to provide a temporary regular residence permit.

We extracted acts from regulatory sources, starting with core-acts, consisting of an ‘action’, an ‘actor’. (that executes the ‘action’), an ‘object’ (that is the target of the ‘action’) and a ‘recipient’ (that receives the ‘result’ of an ‘act’).
Then we added the ‘precondition’ that must be met to have valid’ ‘action’, and the ‘result’ of the ‘action’.

We showed how the ‘precondition’ of an ‘act’ can be elaborated into more detailed interpretations by making ‘fact frames’ that include ‘functions’ from which ‘facts’ can be derived.

All sources used in the interpretations are collected, see section 8.1.2 and 8.2.2, to maintain a trace from the source to the interpretation and vice versa.

We showed how implicit information can be added to the interpretation frames in the annotation slot.

Our experience during the making of these interpretations and during the presentation of the results, suggests that the structured representation of interpretations of regulatory sources is comprehensible for laypersons and that it can yield deeper understanding of the structure of the normative texts for domain experts. 

Further research to substantiate this suspicion scientifically is planned for 2025.

Compared to existing methods for the interpretation of regulatory sources, see chapter 5, the method presented here introduces the following novelties:
    • the use of literal quotes of regulatory sources in interpretation frames,
    • the urge for explication for every deviation from the literal source, and
    • the action-based perspective and the relation of those ‘actions’ to the context in which they are executed and results of the actions.
